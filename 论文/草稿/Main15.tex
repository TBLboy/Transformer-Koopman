\title{Transformer-Koopman: Finite-History Lifting for Controlled Nonlinear Systems}

\author{Bolin Tao}

\markboth{IEEE Journal Draft}{Tao: Transformer-Koopman}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Koopman modeling provides an attractive framework for controlled nonlinear systems by lifting the dynamics into a space where they can be approximated as linear, thereby enabling linear prediction and control design. However, its performance depends critically on the lifting map. Existing approaches, including Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD) and learned lifting methods, typically construct observables from instantaneous states alone, neglecting short-term temporal correlations that are important for identifying accurate linear latent dynamics. To address this limitation, we propose \textbf{Transformer-Koopman}, a temporal lifting framework that encodes a finite history window using a Transformer encoder with non-overlapping multivariate temporal patches. The resulting history-dependent features are combined with the current physical state to form a compact lifted state for Koopman modeling. The associated linear dynamics are identified through a hybrid procedure that jointly trains the encoder and dynamics by gradient descent and then refines the system matrices via closed-form EDMD on the converged latent trajectories. Based on the identified model, we develop a hierarchical LQR controller and establish an ultimate-boundedness guarantee for the closed-loop tracking error, where the bound decreases with the modeling residual. Experiments on a 6-state manipulator platform and a 2-state soft-robot platform show substantially lower rollout error than EDMD and MLP-Koopman baselines, while ablation studies verify the importance of temporal history, patch design, and regularization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Learning predictive models for nonlinear controlled systems is a central problem in system identification and model-based control \cite{ljung1999system, vanoverschee1994n4sid, nelles2020nonlinear}. For control-oriented applications, model quality is determined not only by one-step fit but also by reliable multi-step prediction and compatibility with downstream controller design. The Koopman framework addresses this by lifting nonlinear dynamics into a space where they evolve approximately linearly \cite{koopman1931hamiltonian, brunton2022modern}, enabling the direct application of linear prediction and control tools \cite{otto2021koopman, korda2018linear}.

A central challenge in Koopman modeling is the construction of an accurate and compact lifting map. Existing approaches, including Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD) \cite{williams2015data} and learned lifting methods \cite{lusch2018deep, takeishi2017koopman}, typically construct observables from instantaneous states. This neglects short-term temporal correlations in the state trajectory, forcing the identified linear operator to compensate for missing dynamical context and degrading multi-step prediction accuracy under autoregressive rollout.

To address this limitation, we propose \emph{Transformer-Koopman}, a temporal lifting framework that incorporates trajectory-level information through three coupled design components. First, a finite history window provides explicit temporal context to the lifting map. Second, a compact Transformer encoder with non-overlapping multivariate temporal patches captures inter-step dependencies via self-attention. Third, the resulting history-dependent features are combined with the current physical state to form a structured lifted representation that supports linear latent dynamics and exact state recovery through a fixed projection. The system matrices are identified using a hybrid procedure that combines gradient-based pretraining with a closed-form EDMD refit. Based on the learned model, we design a hierarchical LQR controller and establish an ultimate-boundedness guarantee for the closed-loop tracking error.

The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A temporal lifting framework that constructs Koopman observables from finite history windows using patch-based Transformer encoding, explicitly capturing short-term temporal correlations.
    \item A compact lifted representation with explicit state embedding, enabling exact state recovery and a direct interface for controller design without requiring a nonlinear decoder.
    \item A hybrid identification pipeline combining joint gradient-based training with a closed-form EDMD refinement, validated through trajectory-prediction and ablation studies on two physical platforms.
    \item A hierarchical LQR controller exploiting the lifted-state structure, together with an ultimate-boundedness guarantee linking tracking performance to modeling residual.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related_work} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:problem} formulates the learning problem. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents the proposed method. Section~\ref{sec:control} develops the controller and stability analysis. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} presents experimental results. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.



\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Koopman Modeling: Foundations, Learning, and Control}

Koopman operator theory represents nonlinear dynamics as a linear evolution on observable functions of the state \cite{koopman1931hamiltonian, koopman1932spectra, mezic2005spectral, rowley2009spectral}. In controlled settings, this linear structure enables direct application of standard control synthesis methods. Early extensions such as DMDc \cite{proctor2016dmdc}, input-aware Koopman formulations \cite{proctor2018generalizing, brunton2016invariant}, and Koopman-based MPC \cite{korda2018linear} established this direction, while more recent work incorporates learned representations and provides performance guarantees \cite{han2020deep, shi2022deep, strasser2026overview, duran2025control}. A consistent observation is that controller performance depends critically on model quality, motivating improved lifting design.

In data-driven settings, the Koopman operator is approximated from trajectory data. Classical methods such as EDMD construct a linear predictor over a fixed dictionary of observables \cite{williams2015data, williams2015kernel, klus2018data}. Learned approaches replace hand-crafted dictionaries with data-driven representations, including invariant-subspace models \cite{takeishi2017koopman}, deep autoencoder-based methods \cite{lusch2018deep, yeung2019learning}, and variational formulations \cite{morton2019deep, yuan2022variational}. Extensions incorporating temporal context, such as recurrent Koopman models \cite{otto2019linearly, rank2019recurrent}, suggest that exploiting trajectory information can improve linearization quality. However, most approaches still construct observables from instantaneous states, neglecting short-term temporal correlations.

\subsection{Transformers for Temporal Sequence Modeling}

Transformers \cite{vaswani2017attention} provide a flexible framework for modeling temporal dependencies via self-attention. Efficient variants \cite{zhou2021informer, wang2020linformer, katharopoulos2020transformers, choromanski2021performer}, decomposition-based models \cite{wu2021autoformer, zhou2022fedformer}, and channel-time reformulations \cite{zeng2023transformers, liu2024itransformer} have extended their applicability to time-series data. Patch-based tokenization \cite{nie2023time} further improves efficiency by aggregating local temporal structure before global attention.

Compared to recurrent architectures, self-attention directly models pairwise dependencies across all time steps without sequential compression, making it well suited for capturing unknown temporal structures in dynamical systems. This property is particularly relevant for Koopman lifting, where the appropriate temporal context is not known a priori.

Recent works combine Transformer encoders with Koopman-style latent dynamics for forecasting \cite{forootani2026deepkoopformer, forootani2026spectral}. These approaches integrate sequence modeling and prediction, but often blur the separation between representation learning and linear dynamics.

\subsection{Positioning of the Present Work}
\label{subsec:positioning}

The proposed method connects temporal sequence modeling with control-oriented Koopman lifting. Unlike instantaneous-state lifting methods, it incorporates a finite history window to make short-term temporal correlations explicitly available to the lifting map. Unlike forecasting-oriented Transformer-Koopman hybrids, the Transformer is used solely for history encoding, while all prediction is performed by identified linear latent dynamics. This separation preserves the linear structure required for control synthesis and distinguishes the proposed framework from prior hybrid approaches.


\section{Preliminaries and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:preliminaries_and_problem}

This section reviews the Koopman framework for nonlinear systems, introduces its temporal lifting extension, and formulates the learning problem considered in this work.


\subsection{Koopman Modeling and Temporal Lifting}
\label{sec:preliminary}

Consider a discrete-time controlled nonlinear system
\begin{equation}
    x_{k+1} = F(x_k, u_k),
    \label{eq:nonlinear_system}
\end{equation}
where $x_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the system state, $u_k \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the control input, and $F:\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is an unknown nonlinear transition map. We assume that full-state measurements and the corresponding control inputs are available from recorded trajectory data.

The Koopman operator provides a linear representation of nonlinear dynamics by acting on scalar-valued functions of the state, called observables, rather than on the state itself \cite{koopman1931hamiltonian, mezic2005spectral, rowley2009spectral, brunton2022modern}. An observable $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ is a measurable scalar-valued function of the state. The Koopman operator $\mathcal{K}$ acts on such observables by composition with the system dynamics: for the system \eqref{eq:nonlinear_system}, the Koopman evolution of $\varphi$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    (\mathcal{K}\varphi)(x_k) = \varphi(F(x_k,u_k)),
    \label{eq:koopman_operator}
\end{equation}
Although $F$ is nonlinear, $\mathcal{K}$ is linear on the space of observables: for any observables $\varphi$, $\psi$ and scalars $\alpha$, $\beta$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{K}(\alpha \varphi + \beta \psi)
    = \alpha \mathcal{K}\varphi + \beta \mathcal{K}\psi.
    \label{eq:koopman_linearity}
\end{equation}
This property enables nonlinear dynamics to be represented by a linear evolution in the lifted space, which forms the basis for applying linear analysis and control techniques.

Since the Koopman operator acts on an infinite-dimensional function space, practical computation requires a finite-dimensional approximation. EDMD addresses this by selecting a dictionary of $d$ observables $g_1(x),\ldots,g_d(x)$ and forming the lifted state
\begin{equation}
    z_k = g(x_k) = [g_1(x_k), \ldots, g_d(x_k)]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^d.
    \label{eq:instantaneous_lifting}
\end{equation}
The lifted dynamics are then approximated by a linear system
\begin{equation}
    z_{k+1} \approx A z_k + B u_k,
    \label{eq:edmd_dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}$ are identified from data. 

Given a dataset of $M$ transitions $\{(x_k, u_k, x_{k+1})\}_{k=1}^M$, define $Z = [z_1,\ldots,z_M]$, $Z^+ = [z_2,\ldots,z_{M+1}]$, and $U = [u_1,\ldots,u_M]$. The combined matrix $K = [A\ B]$ is obtained via least squares as
\begin{equation}
    K = Z^+ \Omega^\dagger, \qquad \Omega = [Z\; U],
    \label{eq:edmd_regression}
\end{equation}
where $(\cdot)^\dagger$ denotes the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse. EDMD performance depends critically on the choice of observables; in particular, instantaneous-state liftings neglect short-term temporal correlations, which limits approximation accuracy.

Temporal lifting addresses this limitation by incorporating a finite history of past states into the observable map. For a history length $P$, define the history window
\begin{equation}
    X_k = [x_{k-P+1}^\top, \ldots, x_k^\top]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{P\times n},
    \label{eq:history_window}
\end{equation}
and a temporal lifting map $g_\theta$ that produces the lifted state
\begin{equation}
    z_k = g_\theta(X_k) \in \mathbb{R}^d.
    \label{eq:temporal_lifting}
\end{equation}
This representation incorporates both the current state and recent trajectory context. Motivated by delay-coordinate embedding theory \cite{arbabi2017ergodic, rank2019recurrent}, incorporating temporal context enables the linear latent dynamics
\begin{equation}
    z_{k+1} = A z_k + B u_k + w_k,
    \label{eq:lifted_dynamics_prelim}
\end{equation}
to more accurately approximate the underlying nonlinear system, where $w_k$ denotes the approximation residual. In this work, $g_\theta$ is implemented using a Transformer encoder, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:temporal_lifting}.


\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

Given the system \eqref{eq:nonlinear_system} and the temporal lifting framework defined by \eqref{eq:history_window}--\eqref{eq:temporal_lifting}, the learning problem is formulated as follows.
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D} = \{(X_k, u_k, x_{k+1})\}_{k=1}^{M},
    \label{eq:dataset}
\end{equation}
where each sample consists of a history window $X_k$, the control input $u_k$, and the resulting next state $x_{k+1}$. Only windows that lie within a single trajectory are retained.

The goal is to learn a temporal lifting map $g_\theta$ and identify system matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$, $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times m}$ such that the lifted dynamics
\begin{equation}
    z_{k+1} = A z_k + B u_k + w_k,
    \label{eq:lifted_dynamics}
\end{equation}
approximate the true system evolution with small residual $w_k$ under autoregressive rollout. We seek a compact representation $z_k = g_\theta(X_k)$ that preserves the current physical state in its first $n$ coordinates, while the remaining $d-n$ coordinates encode history-dependent features, enabling exact state recovery through a fixed linear projection and a direct interface for controller design (Section~\ref{sec:control}).

The encoder uses state history only, without past control inputs, and is trained for predictive accuracy under autoregressive rollout, with control compatibility treated as a structural property of the learned model.


% =========================================================
\section{Transformer-Based Temporal Lifting}
\label{sec:methodology}
% =========================================================

In this section, we present the Transformer-Koopman model and its identification procedure. We combine a patch-based Transformer encoder with a structured lifted representation that embeds the physical state and encodes temporal features. We then introduce a three-stage training pipeline integrating gradient-based optimization with a closed-form EDMD refit, and characterize the resulting modeling error.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Temporal Lifting Architecture}
\label{sec:temporal_lifting}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

We construct the lifted state through a patch-based temporal encoding pipeline. A finite history window is first partitioned into non-overlapping multivariate temporal patches, which are then processed by a Transformer encoder to extract history-dependent features. These features are finally concatenated with the current physical state to form a structured lifted representation for Koopman modeling.

Given the history window $X_k \in \mathbb{R}^{P\times n}$, we partition it into $N=P/p$ contiguous non-overlapping patches of temporal length $p$, assuming $p$ divides $P$. Let the $i$th patch be denoted by
\begin{equation}
    X_k^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times n}, \qquad i=1,\ldots,N.
\end{equation}
Each patch contains all $n$ state channels over $p$ consecutive time steps, so the tokenization is jointly multivariate rather than channel-wise. After vectorization,
\begin{equation}
    q_k^{(i)} = \mathrm{vec}(X_k^{(i)}) \in \mathbb{R}^{pn},
\end{equation}
the corresponding token embedding is defined as
\begin{equation}
    e_k^{(i)} = W_p q_k^{(i)} + b_p,
    \label{eq:patch_embedding}
\end{equation}
where $W_p \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}\times pn}$ and $b_p \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{model}}}$ are learnable parameters, and $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ is the Transformer embedding dimension.

Patch tokenization aggregates locally correlated time steps and reduces the token count from $P$ to $P/p$, making self-attention computationally tractable for moderate history lengths. The patch length $p$ therefore controls the tradeoff between local temporal resolution and global modeling efficiency.

The embedded patches are augmented with sinusoidal positional encoding and processed by a Transformer encoder:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{e}_k^{(i)} = e_k^{(i)} + s^{(i)},
\end{equation}
where $s^{(i)}$ denotes the positional encoding associated with token index $i$. Let
\begin{equation}
    H_k = \mathrm{Transformer}\!\left(\bigl\{\tilde{e}_k^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N\right)
    \label{eq:transformer_enc}
\end{equation}
denote the encoded token sequence. A pooling-based readout is then applied,
\begin{equation}
    r_k = \mathrm{Pool}(H_k),
\end{equation}
followed by a linear projection
\begin{equation}
    h_k = W_h r_k + b_h \in \mathbb{R}^{d-n},
    \label{eq:history_feature}
\end{equation}
where $h_k$ is the learned history-dependent feature vector.

The Transformer encoder captures temporal dependencies across patches through self-attention, modeling pairwise interactions over the entire history window without sequential compression.

The lifted state is defined as
\begin{equation}
    z_k = [x_k;h_k] \in \mathbb{R}^{d},
    \label{eq:lifted_state_form}
\end{equation}
where $x_k$ is the final state in the history window and $h_k$ is the encoded temporal feature. This structure preserves the physical state explicitly in the first $n$ coordinates while using the remaining $d-n$ coordinates to encode trajectory-dependent information. The physical state is recovered exactly through the fixed projection
\begin{equation}
    x_k = C z_k, \qquad C = [I_n\;0].
    \label{eq:decoder_projection}
\end{equation}

Given the lifted state \eqref{eq:lifted_state_form}, the latent dynamics are modeled as
\begin{equation}
    z_{k+1} = A z_k + B u_k,
    \label{eq:temporal_dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $A$ and $B$ are identified from data. Equation \eqref{eq:temporal_dynamics} defines the deterministic model used for identification; the associated approximation residual is characterized in Section~\ref{subsec:modeling_error}.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Training Procedure}
\label{sec:training}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

We identify the proposed model through a three-stage procedure that combines neural temporal lifting with closed-form linear regression in the learned latent space. The procedure consists of an initial EDMD estimate, joint gradient-based pretraining of the encoder and linear dynamics, and a final EDMD refit under the converged encoder. The overall pipeline is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:training}.

In the first stage, the encoder is randomly initialized and all training windows are mapped to latent states. An initial estimate of $(A,B)$ is then obtained using the ridge-regularized EDMD regression in \eqref{eq:edmd_solution}, providing a warm start for subsequent optimization.

In the second stage, the encoder and linear dynamics are trained jointly. Define the shifted history window
\begin{equation}
    X_k^{+} = [x_{k-P+2}^\top,\,\ldots,\,x_{k+1}^\top]^\top,
\end{equation}
and the corresponding next latent state
\begin{equation}
    z_k^{+} = g_\theta(X_k^{+}).
\end{equation}
Given the current lifted state $z_k$ and input $u_k$, the predicted next latent and physical states are
\begin{equation}
    \hat{z}_{k+1} = A z_k + B u_k, 
    \qquad
    \hat{x}_{k+1} = C \hat{z}_{k+1}.
\end{equation}
The pretraining objective consists of
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{x} &= \|\hat{x}_{k+1}-x_{k+1}\|_2^2, \label{eq:loss_x}\\
    \mathcal{L}_{z} &= \|\hat{z}_{k+1}-z_k^{+}\|_2^2, \label{eq:loss_z}\\
    \mathcal{L}_{c} &= \bigl\|g_\theta(\mathcal{W}^{+}_{\mathrm{pred}})-z_k^{+}\bigr\|_2^2, \label{eq:loss_c}
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{W}^{+}_{\mathrm{pred}}$ denotes the shifted history window whose most recent entry is replaced by $\hat{x}_{k+1}$. The overall objective is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pre}} = \lambda_x \mathcal{L}_x + \lambda_z \mathcal{L}_z + \lambda_c \mathcal{L}_c.
    \label{eq:pretraining_loss}
\end{equation}
The encoder parameters $\theta$ and the matrices $(A,B)$ are optimized jointly by gradient descent on \eqref{eq:pretraining_loss}.

In the third stage, the encoder parameters are frozen and all latent trajectories are recomputed. The linear dynamics are then refit in closed form using ridge-regularized EDMD. Let
\begin{align}
    Z &= [z_1,\ldots,z_M]^\top, \\
    Z^{+} &= [z_1^{+},\ldots,z_M^{+}]^\top, \\
    U &= [u_1,\ldots,u_M]^\top,
\end{align}
and define
\begin{equation}
    \Omega = [Z\;U].
\end{equation}
The regression is given by
\begin{equation}
    \Theta = (Z^{+})^\top \Omega \left(\Omega^\top \Omega + \lambda I\right)^{-1},
    \label{eq:edmd_solution}
\end{equation}
where $\Theta = [A\;B]$.

After training, the model is deployed autoregressively for multi-step prediction. Starting from an initial history window $X_0$, the lifted state is computed at each step as $z_k = g_\theta(X_k)$, propagated linearly via $\hat{z}_{k+1} = A z_k + B u_k$, and the predicted physical state is recovered as $\hat{x}_{k+1} = C\hat{z}_{k+1}$. The history window is updated by appending $\hat{x}_{k+1}$ and removing the oldest entry. This rollout procedure is used for open-loop evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Transformer-Koopman Training Pipeline}
\label{alg:training}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Dataset $\mathcal{D}$ \eqref{eq:dataset}; hyperparameters $P,p,d,\lambda_x,\lambda_z,\lambda_c,\lambda,E$
\ENSURE Encoder parameters $\theta^{*}$; system matrices $A^{*},B^{*}$

\STATE \textbf{Stage 0: Initial EDMD estimate}
\STATE Initialize $\theta$ randomly; encode all windows to obtain $Z,Z^{+},U$
\STATE $(A,B)\leftarrow$ solve \eqref{eq:edmd_solution}

\STATE \textbf{Stage 1: Joint pretraining}
\FOR{epoch $e=1$ \TO $E$}
    \FOR{each mini-batch}
        \STATE Compute $\hat{z}_{k+1}=Az_k+Bu_k$, $\hat{x}_{k+1}=C\hat{z}_{k+1}$
        \STATE Update $(\theta,A,B)$ via gradient descent on \eqref{eq:pretraining_loss}
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR

\STATE \textbf{Stage 2: Final EDMD refit}
\STATE Fix $\theta$; recompute $Z,Z^{+},U$
\STATE $(A^{*},B^{*})\leftarrow$ solve \eqref{eq:edmd_solution}

\RETURN $\theta^{*}, A^{*}, B^{*}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Modeling Error Characterization}
\label{subsec:modeling_error}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

The identified lifted dynamics are approximate due to both finite-dimensional Koopman truncation and encoder approximation error. We define the one-step modeling error as
\begin{equation}
    w_k = z_{k+1} - (A z_k + B u_k),
    \label{eq:modeling_error}
\end{equation}
where $z_{k+1} = g_\theta(X_{k+1})$. In general, $w_k \neq 0$ even after training.

Similarly, for a reference trajectory with lifted state $z_k^{\mathrm{ref}} = g_\theta(X_k^{\mathrm{ref}})$, the corresponding dynamics satisfy
\begin{equation}
    z_{k+1}^{\mathrm{ref}} = A z_k^{\mathrm{ref}} + B u_k^{\mathrm{ref}} + w_k^{\mathrm{ref}},
    \label{eq:ref_error}
\end{equation}
where $w_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$ denotes the reference modeling error. Both $w_k$ and $w_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$ arise from the same approximation mechanism and will be treated uniformly in the subsequent analysis.




% =========================================================
\section{Controller Design and Stability Analysis}
\label{sec:control}
% =========================================================

In this section, we design a tracking controller based on the identified Koopman model and analyze the resulting closed-loop system. Leveraging the structured lifted state, we construct a hierarchical LQR controller, derive the corresponding tracking error dynamics under modeling error, and establish an ultimate-boundedness result for the closed-loop system.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Controller Design}
\label{subsec:controller_design}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

The identified lifted dynamics admit the structured representation $z_k = [x_k;\, h_k] \in \mathbb{R}^d$, where $x_k \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denotes the physical state and $h_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d-n}$ is the history-dependent feature produced by the encoder. This block structure naturally induces a partition of the tracking error into physical-state and feature components,
\begin{equation}
    e_k^z = z_k - z_k^{\mathrm{ref}} 
    = \begin{bmatrix} e_k^x \\ e_k^h \end{bmatrix}
    = \begin{bmatrix} x_k - x_k^{\mathrm{ref}} \\ h_k - h_k^{\mathrm{ref}} \end{bmatrix},
    \label{eq:structured_error}
\end{equation}
where $z_k^{\mathrm{ref}} = [x_k^{\mathrm{ref}};\, h_k^{\mathrm{ref}}]$ is the lifted reference state and $h_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is obtained by applying the encoder to the reference history window. Since the physical state is recovered exactly via $x_k = C z_k$ with $C = [I_n\;0]$, the physical tracking error satisfies $e_k^x = C e_k^z$.

We assume that a reference trajectory $\{x_k^{\mathrm{ref}}, u_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\}$ is available. The control input is decomposed into feedforward and feedback terms,
\begin{equation}
    u_k = u_{\mathrm{ff},k} + u_{\mathrm{fb},k}.
    \label{eq:ctrl_decomp}
\end{equation}
The feedforward term compensates for the reference evolution in the lifted space,
\begin{equation}
    u_{\mathrm{ff},k} = B^\dagger\!\left(z_{k+1}^{\mathrm{ref}} - A z_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\right),
    \label{eq:feedforward}
\end{equation}
where $B^\dagger$ denotes the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse of $B$. The feedback term is defined as
\begin{equation}
    u_{\mathrm{fb},k} = -K_x e_k^x - K_h e_k^h = -K_c e_k^z,
    \label{eq:hierarchical_fb}
\end{equation}
where $K_c = [K_x\;|\;K_h]$ is partitioned according to \eqref{eq:structured_error}. The resulting control law is
\begin{equation}
    u_k = B^\dagger\!\left(z_{k+1}^{\mathrm{ref}} - A z_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\right) - K_c e_k^z.
    \label{eq:full_ctrl}
\end{equation}

The gain $K_c$ is computed via a discrete-time LQR design for the pair $(A,B)$. We consider the cost
\begin{equation}
    J = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
    \left[(e_k^z)^\top Q\, e_k^z + (u_{\mathrm{fb},k})^\top R\,u_{\mathrm{fb},k}\right],
    \label{eq:lqr_objective}
\end{equation}
where $R \succ 0$ and $Q$ is chosen with a block-diagonal structure
\begin{equation}
    Q = \begin{bmatrix} Q_x & 0 \\ 0 & \alpha Q_h \end{bmatrix}, 
    \qquad \alpha \in [0,1),
    \label{eq:block_diag_Q}
\end{equation}
with $Q_x \succ 0$ and $Q_h \succ 0$. This structure prioritizes physical-state tracking while allowing a tunable penalty on the feature component.

Solving the discrete-time algebraic Riccati equation
\begin{equation}
    A^\top P A - P - A^\top P B(R + B^\top P B)^{-1}B^\top P A + Q = 0
    \label{eq:dare}
\end{equation}
yields the feedback gain
\begin{equation}
    K_c = (R + B^\top P B)^{-1}B^\top P A = [K_x\;|\;K_h].
    \label{eq:lqr_gain}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}
The matrix $Q_x$ determines the primary tracking performance. The parameter $\alpha$ controls the influence of the feature component in the feedback design; setting $\alpha = 0$ removes the penalty on $e_k^h$, while small $\alpha > 0$ introduces a mild auxiliary correction without altering the dominant physical-state objective.
\end{remark}

Because $x_k = C z_k$ holds exactly, tracking performance in the original state space can be evaluated directly from the lifted error $e_k^z$ without requiring a separate observation model. The controller structure is kept fixed across model variants, so that performance differences can be attributed primarily to the learned Koopman model.


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Stability Analysis}
\label{subsec:stability}
% ---------------------------------------------------------

We next analyze the closed-loop tracking error induced by the learned Koopman model and the LQR controller. Substituting \eqref{eq:full_ctrl} into \eqref{eq:lifted_dynamics} and subtracting \eqref{eq:ref_error} yields
\begin{equation}
    e_{k+1}^z = A_{\mathrm{cl}} e_k^z + \delta_k,
    \label{eq:cl_error_dyn}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{cl}} = A - BK_c$ and $\delta_k = w_k - w_k^{\mathrm{ref}}$.

\begin{assumption}[Uniform error bound]
\label{assump:bound}
There exists a constant $\varepsilon \geq 0$ such that
\begin{equation}
    \|w_k\| \leq \varepsilon,
    \qquad
    \|w_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\| \leq \varepsilon
    \label{eq:error_bound}
\end{equation}
for all $k \geq 0$ along the trajectories of interest.
\end{assumption}

Under Assumption~\ref{assump:bound}, one has $\|\delta_k\| \leq 2\varepsilon$ for all $k \geq 0$.

\begin{proposition}[Ultimate boundedness]
\label{prop:ub}
Let $A_{\mathrm{cl}} = A - BK_c$, where $K_c$ is the LQR gain given by \eqref{eq:lqr_gain}. Suppose $(A,B)$ is stabilizable, $(A,Q^{1/2})$ is detectable, and $\|A_{\mathrm{cl}}\| < 1$. Then, under Assumption~\ref{assump:bound}, the lifted tracking error is ultimately bounded:
\begin{equation}
    \|e_k^z\| \leq \frac{2\varepsilon}{1 - \|A_{\mathrm{cl}}\|}
    \label{eq:ub_bound}
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large $k$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Under the stated assumptions, the DARE \eqref{eq:dare} admits a positive-definite solution $P \succ 0$, and $A_{\mathrm{cl}}$ is Schur stable \cite{korda2018linear}. Using the Lyapunov function $V_k = (e_k^z)^\top P e_k^z$ and the closed-loop dynamics \eqref{eq:cl_error_dyn}, one obtains
\begin{equation}
    V_{k+1} - V_k
    \leq
    -\|e_k^z\|_{Q+K_c^\top R K_c}^2
    + 4\varepsilon \|P\|\,\|A_{\mathrm{cl}}\|\,\|e_k^z\|
    + \varepsilon^2 \|P\|.
\end{equation}
Hence $e_k^z$ is ultimately bounded. The explicit bound \eqref{eq:ub_bound} follows by iterating \eqref{eq:cl_error_dyn} with $\|\delta_k\| \leq 2\varepsilon$ and summing the resulting geometric series.
\end{proof}

Since $e_k^x = C e_k^z$ with $C=[I_n\;0]$ and $\|C\|=1$, Proposition~\ref{prop:ub} directly implies
\begin{equation}
    \|x_k - x_k^{\mathrm{ref}}\|
    \leq
    \frac{2\varepsilon}{1 - \|A_{\mathrm{cl}}\|}
    \label{eq:phys_bound}
\end{equation}
for all sufficiently large $k$.

The bound in \eqref{eq:phys_bound} makes explicit the dependence of tracking performance on model quality and closed-loop contraction. In particular, reducing the modeling residual $\varepsilon$ tightens the tracking bound, while smaller $\|A_{\mathrm{cl}}\|$ improves disturbance attenuation.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate the proposed method on two controlled-system datasets, focusing on open-loop trajectory prediction and architectural ablations. In addition, we outline a planned closed-loop trajectory-tracking validation based on the learned Koopman models.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\subsubsection{Datasets}

Experiments are conducted on two real-world systems with different state and input dimensions.

\textbf{6-DoF manipulator system (GeomatricTouch):}  
This system has a 6-dimensional state and 3 control inputs. The dataset contains 15 training trajectories, 4 validation trajectories, and 1 test trajectory, comprising 74,655, 19,909, and 4,976 time steps, respectively.

\textbf{Soft robotic system:}  
This platform has a 2-dimensional state and 2 control inputs. The dataset consists of 49 training trajectories, 21 validation trajectories, and 1 test trajectory, with 24,150, 10,351, and 500 time steps, respectively.

In both datasets, training samples are constructed from history windows that remain within individual trajectories. State and control inputs are normalized using statistics computed from the training set and applied consistently to validation and test data.

\subsubsection{Compared Methods}

We compare three Koopman-based models under a unified evaluation pipeline:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{EDMD:} Classical Koopman approximation with hand-crafted observables.
    \item \textbf{MLP-Koopman:} Learned lifting map based on an MLP encoder using instantaneous states.
    \item \textbf{Transformer-Koopman:} The proposed temporal lifting model based on history windows and patch-based Transformer encoding.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}

All models are evaluated using open-loop autoregressive rollout. Each rollout is initialized with a ground-truth history window from the test trajectory. At each step,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{x}_{k+1} = C(A z_k + B u_k), \quad z_k = g_\theta(X_k),
\end{equation}
and the history window is updated by appending $\hat{x}_{k+1}$ and removing the oldest state.

Performance is measured using trajectory-level RMSE and MAE over the rollout horizon.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented Transformer-Koopman, a temporal lifting framework for learning Koopman models of controlled nonlinear systems from finite history windows. The framework is organized around three coupled technical decisions that form a coherent design chain. First, a finite history window of recent states is used as encoder input rather than the instantaneous state alone, because single-time-step snapshots discard the short-term temporal correlations among successive states that are informative for constructing an accurate linear latent evolution law. Second, the history window is encoded by a Transformer with non-overlapping temporal patch tokenization: patch tokenization aggregates locally correlated time steps before global modeling, and the self-attention mechanism then captures pairwise temporal dependencies across patches without the sequential bottleneck of recurrent architectures. Third, the encoder output is integrated with the current physical state into a compact fixed-dimensional lifted vector, which is directly compatible with the Koopman requirement of finite-dimensional linear dynamics and provides exact physical-state recovery through a fixed projection---avoiding a learned nonlinear decoder and preserving a clean interface for controller design. In this way, the resulting model is not only predictive but also structurally suitable for control-oriented evaluation and later controller design.

The completed experiments support the main identification claim of the paper. On both experimental platforms, the proposed method achieves substantially lower rollout error than the implemented traditional EDMD and MLP-Koopman baselines, consistent with the hypothesis that encoding short-term temporal correlations in the lifting map improves Koopman approximation accuracy. The ablation studies on both platforms confirm that temporal context, patch design, and moderate regularization each contribute meaningfully to model quality, and that removing any of the three ingredients degrades performance in ways that align with their intended roles in the design chain. Together, these results indicate that a more accurate Koopman predictor can be obtained when the lifting map is learned from temporally structured inputs rather than from the current state alone, and that the improvement in modeling residual $\varepsilon$ translates, via the stability analysis of Section~\ref{sec:control}, into a tighter theoretical bound on closed-loop tracking error.

Closed-loop tracking validation is not yet included in the present manuscript. The most direct next step is therefore to compare the learned predictors under a common controller structure and assess whether improved predictive accuracy also translates into improved control performance. A second natural extension is to study richer lifting inputs, including joint state-input histories, within the same control-oriented Koopman framework.
